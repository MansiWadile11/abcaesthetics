What to know before your first Botox® or filler treatment

Neuromodulators, dermal fillers and biostimulators are spoken about as if they were one thing. They are three different categories doing three different jobs, and knowing which is which makes the consultation a great deal easier.

1. Three categories, three different jobs

Almost everything sold as “injectables” falls into one of three groups. They are not interchangeable, they do not address the same concerns, and they do not behave the same way over time.

  • Neuromodulators — Botox®, Dysport®, Xeomin® and Daxxify®. These act on the muscle movement that creates certain lines.
  • Hyaluronic acid fillers — JUVÉDERM®, Restylane® and the RHA® Collections. These add volume where volume has been lost.
  • Biostimulators — Sculptra® and Radiesse®. These work more gradually, by supporting the skin’s own collagen response.

A treatment plan may draw on one category or more than one. Which of them is appropriate for you, if any, is decided at consultation.

2. What a neuromodulator actually does

Some facial lines appear because of repeated muscle movement — frowning, raising the brows, squinting. A neuromodulator temporarily reduces that movement in selected areas, which can soften the appearance of those particular lines.

Two things follow from that. First, it addresses lines associated with movement rather than lines caused by volume loss or sun damage — which is why it is not the answer to every concern. Second, the effect is temporary and wears off gradually, so it is a treatment people return for rather than a one-off.

How long an individual sees an effect for varies, and it is not something a provider can promise in advance.

3. What a hyaluronic acid filler does

Hyaluronic acid is a substance the body already produces. As a filler it is used to restore volume in selected areas, support facial contours, or address specific concerns such as lip shape or definition.

The useful thing about this category is that it is not permanent, and hyaluronic acid fillers can be dissolved if that becomes appropriate. That is a genuine safety feature and worth asking about.

Different products in this family have different handling characteristics, which is part of why product selection is a clinical decision made after assessing the area rather than something chosen from a price list.

4. What a biostimulator does — and why it is slower

Biostimulators are designed to stimulate the skin’s own collagen production and provide gradual improvement in selected areas. They behave differently from a filler: rather than adding volume immediately, they work with the skin’s own response over a period of time.

That means the timeline is different, the number of sessions may be different, and the way you judge the result is different. If you are expecting the same day-one change a filler gives, a biostimulator will read as a disappointment even when it is doing exactly what it is meant to.

5. What happens at the consultation

A first appointment is an assessment, not a treatment slot. Dr. Qneibi will discuss what you are hoping to change, look at the anatomy of the area, review your relevant medical history and any medications or supplements you take, and talk through whether an injectable is an appropriate option at all.

Some concerns are better addressed by something other than an injectable, and some people are not suitable candidates. Being told that is a good outcome from a consultation, not a wasted appointment.

Product selection, whether more than one category is appropriate, and how treatment is spaced are all decided from that assessment rather than in advance.

6. Before you come in

A few general points come up often enough to be worth knowing before you book:

  • Tell the practice about every medication and supplement you take, including over-the-counter ones. Several common supplements affect bruising.
  • Mention any history of cold sores, previous injectable treatments, or filler you have had elsewhere — including anything you are not certain about.
  • If you have an event coming up, say so at the consultation. Timing matters, and it is easier to plan around than to fix afterwards.
  • Come without strong expectations of a specific number or a specific look. The plan follows the assessment.

7. Afterwards, and what “results vary” really means

Aftercare instructions are given at your appointment and are specific to what was done and to you — which is why you will not find them written as a fixed list here.

“Results vary by individual” is not a disclaimer bolted on for legal reasons. Anatomy, skin quality, age, the concern being addressed and how your body responds all differ from person to person. Two people having the same treatment can reasonably have different outcomes, and a provider who tells you otherwise is overselling.

“A first appointment is an assessment, not a treatment slot. Being told an injectable is not the right option for you is a good outcome from a consultation.”

The short version

Work out which of the three categories your concern actually belongs to, come with your history and your questions, and treat the consultation as the place where the plan gets made. That is genuinely the whole of it.

Treatment recommendations, eligibility, treatment frequency, product selection and clinical protocols are individualized following consultation and assessment. Results vary by individual, and no specific outcome is guaranteed. This page is general education, not medical advice.
